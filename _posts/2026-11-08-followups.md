## Terence Tao's beliefs about mathematicians
In [More consistent hashing, or learning math for the advanced in age]({% post_url 2026-02-22-math-and-comp-sci %}), I asked:


> I wish the author of the paper on Terence Tao had asked him what he thought
> mathematicians did. He knew quite a few of them. Did he already appreciate
> that math isn't just given in books, but thought, rethought, argued about, and
> revised? (He probably did.)

This week I was reading the article [The Year Math Changed Forever](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2026/11/09/the-year-math-changed-forever)
in the New Yorker, and came across this bit:

> As a kid, Tao imagined that what professional mathematicians did was solve
> problems assigned to them, sort of like homework. By age sixteen, he was a
> graduate student in the mathematics department at Princeton University, and
> still somewhat in the dark about what he was meant to do with his mathematical
> imagination.


## Retraction: skip _Zen in the Art of Archery_ After noting _Zen in the Art of
Archery_ last week in my post on [the inner game of learning]({% post_url 2026-11-03-inner-game-of-learning %}),
I decided to reread it to see if I still
endorse it. My pdf was janky so I went looking for a better one, and that's when
I came across [The Myth of Zen in the Art of Archery](https://www.kyudo-geneve.ch/kk_en/articles/doc/MythZenArchery.pdf), a
paper by Yamada Shoji.

Eugen Herrigel, the author of _Zen in the Art of Archery_, was significantly
mistaken about many of his experiences in Japan.

> Of course, if Herrigel’s account is considered not as a treatise on Japanese
> archery but merely as his own interpretation of Japanese culture or as his own
> personal story, then it is quite singular and of great interest. Certainly it
> reflects the widespread interest in Japanese Zen that was current at that
> time. When one considers the disparity between actual kyodo and the
> description of Japanese archery that Herrigel presented, however, it is
> impossible to uncritically accept his book as a reliable account of what he
> experienced and observed as a foreigner in Japan.

Herrigel was apparently unaware that Awa, his teacher, was not a Zen
practitioner, and had never studied under a Zen priest. Further, Awa was not an
orthodox archery teacher.

> It is a well-known fact among kyodo researchers that Awa, the person who
> taught Herrigel, was an eccentric instructor.

His eccentricity in the preceding quote is understated.

> As a result, the kyojutsu community treated him \[Awa\] like a lunatic, and on
> occasion people even threw rocks at him when he went to places where
> traditional kyojutsu was firmly entrenched.

Herrigel and Awa used an interpreter, because neither spoke the other's
language. But because of Awa's idiosyncratic way of speaking, the interpreter
confessed to omitting certain contradictory things entirely and otherwise doing
his best to interpret the vague, sometimes nonsensical things Awa would say.
Herrigel read significantly between the lines, and in some cases seems to have
transcribed the spiritual, non-verbal "conversation" that went on between the
student and the teacher.

Also, in spite of Herrigel's interest in Zen, he became in the years following
the publication of the book an enthusiastic Nazi.
