I want to talk about this book, _The Inner Game of Tennis_ by W. Timothy
Gallwey, but somehow without sounding preachy. At it's core it's a book about
sports psychology, with an admixture of self help, and while I like sports
psychology, anything with a whiff of self help makes me uneasy. As a genre, self
help preys on people's nagging feeling that there's some secret out there that
they don't yet know, but can quickly learn, which will solve all of their
problems with effective habits, weight loss, making money, or influencing
people. And there's no secret, not really.

So, I want to talk about this book without pretending like it has some answer to
life's problems. But the fact is, it has the answer to one question that has
been nagging me for a long time. It has to do with breaks.

I like learning new hobbies and skills, especially ones that are technically
challenging: programming, fixing typewriters or motorcycles, observational
sketching, skateboarding, etc. etc. And I'm always in the same pattern with
these hobbies.

1. Learn lots.
2. Practice.
3. Plateau and feel stuck.
4. Get drawn into another hobby or skill.
5. Return after a break to find that I've somehow gotten past my plateau, that I
   can do the thing better than before.

And that last bullet point drives me nuts because it's like I'm unwittingly
improving at something when I'm not doing it at all.

For a time I assumed it was because my brain/nervous system needed a break to
cement good habits, or something. That muscle memory worked better with breaks.
But that just didn't feel like the right answer.

And then I read _The Inner Game of Tennis_, which I had been recommended once or
twice on the internet, and sure enough it breaks the whole thing wide open. This
phenomenon of breaks causing improvement, which I've spent years collecting
evidence for but never had a convincing hypothesis to explain, suddenly felt
understandable.

One of Gallwey's points is that we are "in the zone" when we're both at ease and
alert, with our full attention on the activity we're doing. The zone is fragile
to any lapses in concentration, including ones that are well-meaning. What I
mean is that if you concentrate on form, you're not in the zone. If you
concentrate on the outcome of the match, you're not in the zone. If you're
trying to apply what you've learned, you're at practice, not in the zone.

Moreover, any attention spent on worrying about your self-image or negative
consequences is attention that is taken away from the activity itself. In
tennis, you shift from "Can I return this serve?" to "What if I can't return
this serve?"

What's always bothered me is my sense that after some time spent learning, I
feel like I _should_ have improved by now. If I haven't learned, then I've been
messing up. So I look for evidence of improvement, I lose focus, and I falter.

Or, another failure mode: I've read books, watched videos, and picked up lots of
little tips for each part of my technique. All this advice rattles around in my
head, encouraging my ego to use some of it so it doesn't go to waste. I forget
my real goal, I lose focus, and I falter.

I take a break for a few weeks or months, get invested in something else, and
forget everything I know about hobby X for a bit. And then my interest gets
sparked again and I pick it up, only to find my mind clear, my worries gone, the
little technical tips silent. I switch from asking myself, "Have I gotten
better?" to "Remember what you like so much about this?"

It's no wonder I do the thing better than before!

Now... how do you harness that so you can do well _without_ taking a long break?
That's the real subject of Gallwey's book. So that's my recommendation -- to all
those who are stuck, cursed with knowing the right idea but not being able to
execute it ("Head down!"), or are simply not having fun, take a read through
this book and see if it doesn't help.

Some other recommended reading:

- _Zen in the Art of Archery_. Classic text on zen performance (from which
  Gallwey takes a large influence).
- [Ratings Fear and Loathing](https://www.pathtochessmastery.com/2011/10/ratings-fear-and-loathing.html).
Great blogpost about chess ratings in particular. It's hepled me a lot with my
rating worries when I play Go. The rest of this blog is great, too, for brain
sport psychology in general. For a look at how chess and tennis in particular
are linked, check out [this series of posts](https://www.pathtochessmastery.com/search?q=chess+vs+tennis)
- _Harvey Penick's Little Red Book_. No one knows suffering like golfers. The
  Golf Channel is the only channel I've seen where in between commercials, they
sometimes give a quick golf lesson. I read Harvey Penick's book when I was a
teen golfer and it helped me a lot with understanding how to apply advice, how
to focus ("Take dead aim!"), and, incidentally, with my short game.

And some other things that Gallwey's book helped me understand:

- Verbal advice is rarely helpful.
- A bad habit is simply a desperate, ineffective solution to a problem. If you
  find a better solution, bad habits disappear quite easily. (In school, I found
it easy to change how I wrote certain letters if I found a form I liked better.
All I did every day was write, so if habits were hard to break, this should've
been impossible.)
- I was asked to design the cocktail napkin for my friend's wedding -- a sketch
  of her two bunny rabbits. I'm emphatically not a professional artist, but I
accepted. I did dozens of drafts without finding one that I liked, and the
pressure seemed like it was getting to me. And one day I went to a cafe with my
wife, Bre, and drew a pair of sketches of the buns, directly and in pen, in a
realistic style unlike my previous line drawings. I frankly don't remember what
was going through my head when I was doing them, probably nothing much. And that
was the design that ended up on the napkin.

And now, to wrap things up, a zen story.

> When one goes to Obaku temple in Kyoto he sees carved over the gate the words
> “The First Principle.”
> 
> The letters are unusually large, and those who appreciate calligraphy always
> admire them as being a masterpiece. They were drawn by Kosen two hundred years
> ago.
>
> When the master drew them he did so on paper, from which workmen made the
> larger carving in wood. As Kosen sketched the letters a bold pupil was with
> him who had made several gallons of ink for the calligraphy and who never
> failed to criticize his master’s work.
>
> “That is not good,” he told Kosen after the first effort.
> 
> “How is that one?”
> 
> “Poor. Worse than before,” pronounced the pupil.
> 
> Kosen patiently wrote one sheet after another until eighty-four First
> Principles had been accumulated, still without the approval of the pupil.
> 
> Then, when the young man stepped outside for a few moments, Kosen thought:
> “Now is my chance to escape his keen eye,” and he wrote hurriedly, with a mind
> free from distraction. “The First Principle.”
> 
> “A masterpiece,” pronounced the pupil.
