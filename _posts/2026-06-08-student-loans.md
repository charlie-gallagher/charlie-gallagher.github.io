My high school Latin teacher gave me some advice about picking an undergraduate
school: follow the money. I found a good private school that was going to let me
study for half price, which came to a mild $30K a year.

After some financial ups and downs, I ended up taking almost the entire bill on
credit through both government and private student loans. I worked during
summers and winters doing shift work and played music at small venues, but that
was only enough to pay for my books and food. I found myself in debt up to my
ears by senior year.

Even by the second half of my freshman year, the weight of my chosen school's
$120K sticker price (assuming I got out in four years) started to bear down on
me, and I fell into a harsh depression for about 7 months. Student loans are
aggressively managed in the United States. The interest rates for private loans
can be as high as 14%, and no type of student loan can be discharged during
bankruptcy. (This apparently comes from the politically popular but
sociologically dubious idea that students will take out hella student loans, and
then purposefully default on them to get a free education.) At those interest
rates, with such harsh legal treatment, student loans can quickly spiral into a
debt that you cannot and will not ever pay off. Caveat to those 17-year-old
emptors, poor things.

I eventually chose to study economics, the idea being that if I didn't know what
I was going to do, at least I ought to be good at making decisions about it. For
a research project, one professor suggested I study the outcomes of student loan
borrowers.

While researching student loan borrower outcomes, two things happened. First, I
found that the "typical" case of borrowers who take out more than $100K in loans
(like me) is relatively rare, and that the outcomes for this tranche are
surprisingly good. Low default rates, high employment rates, and so on. High
debt is usually an indicator of extensive education and/or a high quality
institution. This eased some of my anxiety about my loans.

Second, I got a job. I applied for an internship at the Federal Reserve Bank of
Philadelphia, and a credit risk researcher named Silvio Rendon was interested in
continuing my student loan outcomes research. I later found out that compared to
many of the other interns, I was underqualified. Quite noticeably
underqualified. It was on the basis of my student loan research alone then that
I ended up getting the position. And at the same time I began earnestly
assessing my own loan burdens in light of the research. I would have to make a
$1300 payment every month for the next 20 years or risk penury (or at least mild
financial harm, like garnished wages). So, steep, but nothing more than a rent
payment...  I could figure it out.

While on that internship I learned R and ggplot and began participating in Tidy
Tuesday, a weekly Twitter gathering of data visualization geeks. At some points,
my intership was part time, and I spent the rest of my time fiddling with R code
and making these graphics. That portfolio of visualizations hepled me get my
next job, at IXIS, where I've now worked for five years.

So that's the little through line of my student loans: depression, research,
opportunity, career. I've been working on paying them off in large chunks as my
savings would allow, and on and on until last year, when I realized that I had
become net positive for the first time since I began college in 2015.  Strictly
speaking, I could've paid off my student loans last fall, if I were willing to
take the risk of having zero savings. I used my better judgment and made a plan
instead to pay off everything by my 30th birthday, which is coming up in a week
or two.

Today settled my last account, $8K paid to EdFinancial Services. I still have to
tally up how much I ultimately paid for college, but I'm sure it was over $130K.
Would I do it again? I would. I wouldn't _like_ to, but this path led to a
career that I'm passionate about. I can imagine a case where things turned out
differently, but fortunately, I don't have to consider that anymore. Me and the
bursar's office, we've parted ways. Good riddance, and good luck.
