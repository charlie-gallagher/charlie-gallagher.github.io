Hello again! I hope you'll excuse one more departure from talking about
programming. I've been learning to play go lately and it's sent me down this
whole path of sports and learning psychology.

I was talking with my brother yesterday about some of the "hard" hobbies we
have: learning a language, observational drawing, go (baduk), golf. Something
they all share is a high likelihood that you will, at some point, develop a bad
habit or two. We were talking about _The Inner Game of Tennis_ and what sorts of
bad habits there are, how you break them.

Up front: A bad habit is a crude and safe solution to a problem. If you find a
better solution the bad habit will quietly and permanently die.

Example bad habits:

- Lifting your head up during a golf swing.
- Drawing what you think you see, rather than what's actually there.
- In inflected languages like Czech or Latin, being slapdash with your use of
  cases.
- Using some "ungrammatical" expression or usage, like "Bob and me are going to
  the store."
- In go or chess, using crude forcing moves.

Here's W. Timothy Gallwey on bad habits:

> It is not helpful to condemn our present behavior patterns -- in this case our
> present imperfect strokes -- as "bad"; it is helpful to see what function
> these habits are serving, so that if we learn a better way to achieve the same
> end, we can do so. We never repeat any behavior which isn't serving some
> function or purpose. It is difficult to become aware of the function of any
> pattern of behavior while we are in the process of blaiming ourselves for
> having a "bad habit." But when we stop trying to suppress or correct the
> habit, we can see the function it serves, and then an alternative pattern of
> behavior, which serves the same function better, emerges quite effortlessly.
>
> ...
>
> Because these \[bad habit\] patterns are serving a function, the behavior is
> reinforced or rewarded and tends to continue. The deeper the groove in the
> nervous system, the harder it seems to be to break the habit. We have all had
> the experience of deciding that we will not hit a tennis ball a certain way
> again.
>
> ...
>
> In short, there is no need to fight old habits. Start new ones. It is the
> resisting of an old habit that puts you in that trench. Starting a new pattern
> is easy when done with childlike disregard for imagined difficulties.

I'm a believer in this line of thinking now, especially after reflecting on some
of my own changes in behavior.

Interesting thing \#1: bad habits don't depend on repetition. You can pick up a
bad habit with very little repetition, and you can break a bad habit even if
you've done it a thousand times. I've certainly had the experience of picking up
a bad habit almost instantly. And I've made almost instant changes to my
handwriting, and if there's a more automatic skill than handwriting, I haven't
found it. If I can change how I write the letter "r" to a form I like better,
there's no habit I can't break.

Interesting thing \#2: bad habits feel safe, and they feel safe because they're
somehow effective. The problem tends to be that they're effective in the wrong
direction. You lift your head up while swinging a golf club because it helps you
find out how well you hit the shot and where it's going; it doesn't help you hit
more accurately. In your mind, the swing is a period of uncertainty, and you
want to know how it turned out.

Interesting thing \#3: hiking up the wrong mountain. There's a story of a
climber who wanted to climb the tallest mountain in the world. He found two
enormous peaks, side-by-side, but their peaks were above the clouds, and he
couldn't see from the ground which one was the tallest. So he picked the one
that looked a bit larger and started climbing, only to find that when he reached
a point above the clouds, the other peak was in fact much taller. You'll hear
this story in academia because it rhymes with the level of commitment it takes
to pursue a line of research, and the risk that after years of investment,
you'll find that the other peak was indeed much taller. The same parable applies
to habits, and this story I'm pulling from the excellent podcast [Sold a
Story](https://features.apmreports.org/sold-a-story/). Children who learn to
read with three cueing first invest time into learning to "read" this way, only
to find out later that their reading does not improve. Unfortunately for these
children, the only way to reach a higher peak is to start from scratch with
phonics. Three cueing isn't just a bad habit, it's a failed ascent, or rather an
ascent that is quite limited. Their cueing experience will continue to be more
effective than the phonics-based techniques for a long time, because the fact
is, they need to read. Phonics takes time, and cueing may be limited, but it
gets the job done. In circumstances like that, it's very hard to break out of
three cueing.

The point of all this is that bad habits are different than I thought, and
different than you might think. They are safe solutions to problems that need a
solution, but they're not really effective. What's harder to accept is that if
you just find/learn a better solution, the bad habit disappears naturally and
without shame or fanfare. It gets left behind like an old letterform for "r"
that I no longer like.

Here's the same list of bad habits from the beginning of the post, and some
comments.

- **Lifting your head up during a golf swing.** Makes your swing inconsistent,
  but gives you the earliest glance at the quality of your shot.
- **Drawing what you think you see, rather than what's actually there.** Betty
  Edwards addresses this better than I can. But in short, it comes from a lack
of trust in your ability to translate what you see into a convincing picture.
- **In inflected languages like Czech or Latin, being slapdash with your use of
  cases.** Comes from a lack of belief that cases are _necessary_ for good
communication. If you can get by without perfect usage of cases, you'll never
feel the need to sharpen them up.
- **Using some "ungrammatical" expression or usage, like "Bob and me are going to
  the store."** This one's interesting. My take is that you can easily fall into
a habit of _correcting_ your own grammar rather than speaking with correct
grammar in the first place. (And by grammar, I really mean conventionally
correct usage.) The language part of the brain is responsible for communication,
so if saying 'Bob and me' is good enough for perfect communication, it's happy.
But the editor part of the brain may jump in a second too late and say "Wait,
that's not right." The editor feels like it's improper, but the communicator
isn't convinced. So it sits in limbo.
- **In go or chess, using crude forcing moves.** Forcing moves feel safe,
  because you know the response. It makes the game feel predictable and in
control, but those moves aren't helping you advance towards _winning_. For that,
you need strategy and experience, both of which come harder than recognizing a
forcing move.
